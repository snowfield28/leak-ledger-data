Key Takeaways
A Microsoft 365 credential (API secret) belonging to ConnectWise Cloud Backup (formerly SkyKick) has been compromised, and Huntress has confirmed it is being used to gain unauthorized access to customers' M365 environments.
Huntress has high confidence that the threat actor abused ConnectWise Cloud Management applications for Exchange Online, Microsoft Teams, and SharePoint Online, Cloud Backup, MS Graph, and Partner Center.
In one Huntress partner tenant, a second ConnectWise application, 
ConnectWise Cloud Management - MS Teams
 (Application ID 
24205314-2b89-4e07-a2a4-579e898a5640
), gave itself Global Administrator and then added new credentials to two other applications in that tenant, one of them being a single-tenant Huntress application unique to this partner.
Microsoft's sign-in logs record the second application connection coming from a private IPv6 address (ULA) 
fde4:8dba:2600:37ce:6d26:200:a76:*
, a range Microsoft logs for traffic inside its own cloud network. Legitimate ConnectWise traffic and unrelated customer traffic also appears to originate from the same ULA, so please consider the address alone does not identify who operated the system. We are working to determine that.
Disabling M365 apps, including the abused ConnectWise apps will not remove credentials that were already added. You must review the credentials on all of your M365 applications.
Impacted organizations should immediately 
disable 
the 
ConnectWise Cloud Backup
 (formerly SkyKick, Application ID: 
506e08f9-6e9a-4da4-942b-a099a03644ae
) and 
ConnectWise Cloud Management - MS Teams 
(Application ID 
24205314-2b89-4e07-a2a4-579e898a5640
) applications in their Microsoft 365 tenant. Consider temporarily disabling other ConnectWise Cloud Management application IDs as well.
Disable application IDs, do not delete them. Disabling the app in your tenant does not change backups ConnectWise has already stored. Confirm retention and restore options with ConnectWise.
Do not re-enable affected ConnectWise applications until ConnectWise confirms all credentials have been reset.

What happened after access was gained: 
Since our last update, Huntress has identified a second tenant with post-compromise activity. Huntress was not deployed on this tenant originally, but we saw service principal sign-in attempts 
from
 that tenant to Huntress-protected tenants (from the 
*.128
 IP address). Working proactively with a partner, we were able to get Huntress deployed to this second tenant in order to collect additional telemetry.
In the two tenants with this activity, the post-compromise behavior was fast, scripted, and notably indiscriminate. In both tenants, the post-compromise activities took place within two minutes of each other around 03:45 UTC on October 15. The procedure was the same each time: the application granted itself Global Administrator, then planted a new secret on a small number of other applications in the tenant to keep a foothold. The choice of which applications to backdoor appeared to be random in the two tenants we reviewed. Across the two compromised tenants, the identical grant flow selected two completely different sets of applications. Notably, one application existed in BOTH tenants but was chosen for secret planting in only one of them. In one tenant, the applications that received planted secrets were among the least privileged in the environment, while far more powerful applications sat untouched. The only consistent criteria we can point to is structural: credentials were planted in applications that are registered inside the affected tenant, without regard to what those applications can actually do.
Just as important is what we did not see: The access may have been used for Microsoft Graph directory reads, which leave no audit record. In the two tenants we reviewed, we also found no evidence of mailbox or file manipulation, or user or policy changes.
Our surgical response:
 You might be wondering why our response did not include mass disabling the ConnectWise/SkyKick apps across 
every
 tenant. Our approach to containment is deliberate, surgical, and evidence-based, and this incident called for a scalpel, not a hammer. We prioritized the tenants with confirmed successful unauthorized sign-ins, and our guidance for everyone else has been to disable the affected apps and check their logs.
When our investigation kicked off, we quickly established a clear picture of the attack window. The malicious application sign-ins were tightly bunched across the morning of October 15 UTC, and the last confirmed successful sign-in we observed with this credential was at 08:44 UTC on October 15. After that, we saw only failed attempts in the tenants where we can see sign-in logs. With evidence in hand that there was no current or repeated activity (in the tenants where we can see sign-in logs), it simply didn't make sense to create a larger-than-necessary blast radius.
This judgment call allowed our experts and agents to focus closely on environments with confirmed unauthorized sign-ins, share what we found with industry partners, and publish guidance for the community.
We believe it's these surgical moments our partners most appreciate and count on when 
it
 hits the fan, especially when the wrong decision can disrupt working systems and business productivity as collateral damage.

Background
ConnectWise Cloud Backup (Formerly SkyKick) is a cloud-based backup and recovery service built for managed service providers (MSPs) to protect their clients' Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace data. To do that job, the app is granted broad access to mailboxes, OneDrive, SharePoint, Teams, and directory data in every tenant it is installed in. 
On October 15, the application's identity signed in to customer Microsoft 365 tenants from 
144.172.64[.]128
, a hosting provider address, and received Microsoft Graph access tokens. This is not how the app normally behaves: legitimate traffic comes from Azure private IP space. As of 19:00 UTC on October 16, Huntress has confirmed unauthorized sign-ins in 162 tenants.
The authentication itself is certain: It comes from Microsoft's own sign-in logs in each tenant. The credential type was a client assertion, which means the credential lives in the vendor's own tenant. Nothing a customer did in their environment could have produced it. 
ConnectWise has 
acknowledged the incident
 on its website and confirmed the IP is associated with unauthorized activity. Todyl has also
 reported on this incident
.

What Happened
What do we know?
 The app's credential is in hostile hands. It was used to obtain Microsoft Graph tokens in customer tenants from 
144.172.64[.]128
 on October 15, between 06:13 and 08:34 UTC. The actor moved through tenants in sorted GUID order, a few seconds apart, which points to a script working from a target list.
Who is affected?
 Any organization with the app installed. The credential works against every tenant the app is consented in, so the actor is not limited to the tenants we have confirmed so far.
How does it work?
 The attacker authenticates to the app using their own tooling, the same way the backup service does, and gets tokens with the app's existing permissions. 
What are the potential repercussions?
 Because backup and restore both need to read and write data, those permissions are broad. An attacker holding those tokens can read the same mailboxes, files, and directory data the backup service can.
Huntress observations
Here is the timeline of what Huntress has seen from the same infrastructure:
October 13 to 14
: Password spraying against four law firm tenants from 
144.172.64[.]128
. Scripted OAuth2 token requests (User-Agent
 curl/7.88.1
) from datacenter infrastructure. Two accounts were locked out by smart lockout. We saw no successful user sign-ins from this IP in Huntress telemetry.
October 15, from 01:58 UTC: 
In roughly 50 tenants monitored by Huntress, service principals for ConnectWise's Cloud Management apps (Exchange Online, Microsoft Teams, and Microsoft Graph) assigned themselves 
directory
 roles, mostly Exchange Administrator. The User-Agent on these events (
kiota-dotnet
) is the one ConnectWise's own apps use, which points to vendor automation, but we have not confirmed that with ConnectWise.
October 15, 03:44 to 03:49 UTC, one customer tenant: 
The ConnectWise Cloud Management app for Microsoft Teams (application ID 
24205314-2b89-4e07-a2a4-579e898a5640
) added itself to the Global Administrator role, then ran a Python script (User-Agent 
Python-urllib/3.12
) that modified two other applications in the tenant: an MSP management application, and Huntress's own application (Huntress Security Platform), adding new client secrets to both. A followup attempt to add a certificate failed. The new secrets were set to expire in 2028.
October 15, 06:13 to 08:34 UTC: 
The app authenticated from 
144.172.64[.]128
 in 17 of the first 290~ tenants Huntress checked, across 17 different partner accounts.
Between October 13 and 14: 
The same address also made a scripted sign-in attempted using client application IDs belonging to other tools.
In the tenant where we saw persistence, the entire sequence took about five minutes. The ConnectWise Cloud Management app for Microsoft Teams added itself to Global Administrator at 03:44 UTC and again at 03:48. In the same window it made nine changes to two other applications, an MSP management application and Huntress's own application. Six of the changes succeeded and three failed. One failure was a certificate credential that Microsoft rejected with a thumbprint mismatch, and the client secrets that were added afterward succeeded.
A client secret and a certificate are two ways for an application to prove its identity to Entra, and either one gives the holder the same access: sign in as that application, from anywhere, using the permissions already granted to it. App-only sign-ins involve no user, no device, and no MFA prompt. The person who creates a secret sees its value at creation, so the attacker holds working credentials for both applications until someone removes them. Adding credentials to an existing application is a known persistence technique (MITRE ATT&CK T1098.001, 
Account Manipulation: Additional Cloud Credentials
).
The two applications that received new secrets are trusted, privileged tools. Adding a credential to an MSP management application can extend access beyond the one tenant, because those applications are often consented into the MSP's customers' tenants. A credential on a security tool's application gives the holder whatever that tool is permitted to do.
The Global Administrator change and the credential additions happened about two and a half hours before the first app authentications from 
144.172.64[.]128
 at 06:13 UTC. We have no source address for the earlier activity, so we have not tied the two phases to the same operator. User-Agents helped us distinguish vendor activity from attacker activity. The ConnectWise Cloud Management and Partner Center applications we reviewed make their Graph calls with a .NET SDK User-Agent (
kiota-dotnet
). The application changes in the one tenant came from a Python User-Agent, and the password spray used
 curl/7.88.1
. None of these is unique to this actor, and Python and curl are common in ordinary administration, so 
the combination of actor, operation, and User-Agent is the useful signal
, not any single one.
Microsoft's audit log records role assignments and application changes along with the acting application, but for app-only actions it carries no source IP, and it does not record the details of the credential that was added or what an application read through Microsoft Graph. Confirming exactly what was added in a tenant takes the Entra audit log or a Graph query for that tenant, which is why we ask partners to review credentials directly.
What we have not confirmed
Impact. 
In the one tenant audit log we reviewed in detail, the actor shows no mail or file access. That does not mean no data was taken. Directory reads through Microsoft Graph leave no audit trail, so "tokens issued, nothing visible taken" is the current state.
Secondary app exposure.
 ConnectWise has multiple applications with very high-level permissions. In one customer tenant, Huntress confirmed that a second ConnectWise application, ConnectWise Cloud Management for Microsoft Teams, was used to grant itself Global Administrator and add credentials to other applications. We cannot verify that the remaining ConnectWise applications are secure.
What to do next
Disable the application now, even if you see nothing unusual.
In the Microsoft Entra admin center, go to Enterprise applications and search for Application ID 
506e08f9-6e9a-4da4-942b-a099a03644ae
 (you can also search for "SkyKick" or "ConnectWise Cloud Backup").
Open Properties and set "Enabled for users to sign-in" to No.
Do not delete the application.
 Deleting it removes the consent, and you will need to re-consent to restore service.
Check for other ConnectWise and SkyKick applications in your tenant (search for "SkyKick" and "ConnectWise"). If you cannot verify one is safe, disable it as well, without deleting, until ConnectWise confirms that it is.
